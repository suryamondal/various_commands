\section{The silent-failure catalogue}
\label{sec:silent}

This is the most transferable chapter in the report.

Over the course of this deployment, \textbf{eleven separate defects shared one
shape}: the configuration parsed cleanly, the diagnostic tools reported correct
values, and the feature did nothing. Not one of them produced an error. Several
produced a pool that looked, by every command an operator would naturally run,
completely healthy.

\begin{keypoint}
\kn{condor\_config\_val} and \kn{condor\_status} never revealed a single one of
them. Both tools answer questions that are not the question you are asking:
\kn{condor\_config\_val} reports what the configuration \emph{says},
\kn{condor\_status} reports what the collector was \emph{last told}. Neither
reports what the machine will \emph{do}.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Why this system produces this failure shape}

Three properties combine, and each is individually reasonable.

\textbf{ClassAd evaluation is total.} There is no type error and no exception. An
expression referencing a missing attribute yields \kn{UNDEFINED}, and
\kn{UNDEFINED} propagates through \kn{\&\&} to produce a non-true result. The
policy does not fail; it declines, forever, quietly.

\begin{trap}
\kn{UNDEFINED} is \textbf{worse than false}. A false \kn{START} parks the slot in
\st{Owner} state, which is visible in one column of \kn{condor\_status}. Undefined
leaves it displaying \st{Unclaimed/Idle} --- the exact appearance of a healthy
machine waiting for work.
\end{trap}

\textbf{The layers are decoupled by design.} Configuration parsing, ad
publication, collector state and startd decision-making are four separate stages
with a time delay between each. A tool that inspects one stage tells you nothing
about the next. This is what makes the system robust in operation and opaque in
diagnosis.

\textbf{Declining work is a legitimate state.} The whole point of the owner
policy is that machines refuse jobs. There is therefore no threshold of refusal
at which the system concludes something is wrong --- refusing everything forever
is, to Condor, a machine whose owner is very busy.

\subsection{The verification discipline that follows}

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{Verify on the slot ad, and verify under load.} Those are the only two
checks that discriminate between a working policy and a dead one. Everything
else confirms your intent back to you.
\end{keypoint}

\subsubsection{Check the ad, not the configuration}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=term]
condor_status -af Name Start OwnerSessionActive HostMemAvailMB HostDiskAvailMB
\end{lstlisting}

If an attribute the policy depends on is absent here, the policy is dead
regardless of what \kn{condor\_config\_val} prints. Allow for the update
interval, or force a fresh ad with \kn{condor\_reconfig}.

\subsubsection{Check that a job lands, not that the machine appears}

Appearing in \kn{condor\_status} and accepting work are independent properties.
Four rows of the catalogue above are machines that appeared perfectly and
accepted nothing. The acceptance test is a job that runs to completion on that
specific machine.

\subsubsection{Apply real load}

Suspension, eviction and admission closure cannot be verified by inspection ---
all three failed silently under inspection and were found only under load. Raise
load in stages so a crossing can be attributed to a known input, and bound every
generator with \kn{timeout} so nothing survives a dropped session.

\subsubsection{Read StartLog}

When the ad looks right and jobs still do not land, the startd's own log is the
only place the decision is recorded. \kn{Request to claim resource refused} and
\kn{Claiming protocol failed} appear there and nowhere else.

\begin{keypoint}
Before concluding a policy is broken, confirm the instrument produced the input
you believe it did. In three of these four cases the system was behaving
correctly for the input it actually received.
\end{keypoint}
